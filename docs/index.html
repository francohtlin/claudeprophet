How forecasting works

Kalshi lists a company KPI not as one contract but as a ladder of threshold contracts — “will this company report revenue above $40 billion in Q3 2026?”, repeated at a dozen different thresholds. ClaudeProphet collapses each ladder back into the single number it is really about, has Claude research that number, and then converts one probability distribution into a price for every rung of the ladder. Those per-threshold probabilities are what this dashboard puts side by side with the market.

Every step is a plain Python script in forecasting/; only step 4 calls a model.

Step by step

  1. Pull the open markets. forecasting/pull_kpi_markets.py walks every KPI-tagged Kalshi series in the Companies and Financials categories and records each open contract — question, close time, current mid price, volume — into data/company_kpi_open.jsonl.
  2. Group contracts into metrics. forecasting/select_kpi.py parses each question into a (threshold, metric, period) triple and groups the contracts by (company, metric, period, resolve date). One group = one threshold ladder = exactly one number that has to be forecast.
  3. Keep only what is genuinely uncertain. The market-implied median is the threshold at which the market’s P(Yes) crosses 50%, interpolated between the two rungs that straddle it. If that crossing falls inside the ladder the metric is uncertain and worth forecasting; if the entire ladder is already priced above or below, the market has effectively settled it and it is dropped. Metrics that were already forecast are skipped, the survivors are ranked by traded volume, and the top N are written to data/forecasts/_chosen.json.
  4. Claude researches each chosen metric. forecasting/forecast_kpi.py spawns one separate claude -p child process per metric (model claude-opus-4-8, up to 40 turns, web search enabled). The prompt asks it to find the most recent company guidance, the current analyst/consensus estimate, the prior-quarter and year-ago actuals for that same metric, and the recent growth trend. It is told the market’s implied central estimate and explicitly instructed not to copy it. The child must reply with strict JSON: {median, p10, p90, reasoning, key_evidence}.
  5. Turn three numbers into a distribution. The p10/p90 pair is read as the 80% central interval of a normal distribution, so the standard deviation is recovered as sigma = (p90 - p10) / 2.5631. The forecast is then the normal distribution centred on the median with that sigma.
  6. Price every rung of the ladder. For each threshold t in that metric’s ladder the script evaluates P(value ≥ t) = 1 - Φ((t - median) / sigma) and stores the result in cp_thresholds. A single researched view of the number therefore produces a full, internally consistent set of contract probabilities.
  7. Append and compare. Each record — forecast, thresholds, reasoning, evidence, cost and web-search counts — is appended to data/forecasts/open_kpi_claudeprophet.jsonl. The dashboard joins that back onto the live market snapshot and shows, contract by contract, ClaudeProphet’s probability next to the market’s price for the same threshold.

The core of it: one distribution, many contracts

The whole method rests on a single conversion. Claude gives three numbers; those three numbers define a bell curve; the area of that curve to the right of a threshold is the probability that the contract settles Yes.

sigma = (p90 − p10) / 2.5631
P(value ≥ t) = 1 − Φ( (t − median) / sigma )

The constant 2.5631 is the width of the 10th-to-90th percentile band of a standard normal distribution, measured in standard deviations (2 × 1.2816). Dividing the forecast’s p10–p90 spread by it recovers sigma, and Φ is the standard normal CDF. A wider p10–p90 means a less confident forecast, which flattens every probability on the ladder toward 50%.

The shaded tail is exactly the probability quoted for that threshold contract. Every rung of the ladder is a different vertical line on the same curve.

Reading the Dashboard tab

  • Market est. — the market-implied central value: where the ladder’s P(Yes) crosses 50%.
  • ClaudeProphet — the researched median, with the p10–p90 range shown when a row is expanded.
  • Edge — how far the ClaudeProphet median sits from the market-implied one, as a percentage of the metric. A large edge means the two disagree about the underlying number, not merely about one contract.
  • Expanded rows — the reasoning Claude returned plus the full ladder: market price as the bar, the ClaudeProphet probability as the tick mark on the same bar.
  • Track record and Paper portfolio — Brier scores against settled outcomes, and a $1,000 paper bankroll that takes the single largest disagreement per metric. Paper only; nothing is traded.

Caveats worth knowing

  • The distribution is assumed normal and symmetric. Real KPI outcomes are often skewed, so the far tails of the ladder are the least trustworthy part of the output.
  • The p10/p90 come from a language model’s self-assessed uncertainty, which is the main thing the track record is there to test.
  • Market prices are mid-quotes from the latest snapshot, not executable prices, and they move after the forecast is made.

Trading strategy

Once a metric has been forecast, ClaudeProphet has a probability for every rung of that metric’s threshold ladder, and the market has a price for every rung too. The strategy is deliberately narrow: for each forecasted metric it takes one position, on the single contract where the two disagree most. Everything lives in forecasting/portfolio.py, the ledger is data/portfolio.json, and it is paper only — nothing is ever traded, no broker is contacted, no order is placed.

Illustrative ladder for one metric. The bar is the market’s mid, the tick is ClaudeProphet’s probability for the same threshold, the shaded band between them is the divergence. Exactly one rung — the widest band — becomes a position.

What the code actually does

  1. Collect the rungs for one metric. contract_rows_for() pulls every open contract that belongs to the same (company, metric, period, resolve date) group as the forecast, and parses each question back into its numeric threshold.
  2. Compare our probability against the market at each rung. The forecast’s cp_thresholds are indexed by threshold, so every contract yields a pair: cp_p (our probability) and yes_mid (the market’s mid). The divergence is simply diff = cp_p - yes_mid.
  3. Throw away the near-settled tails. A rung whose mid sits outside PRICE_BAND = (0.03, 0.97) is skipped before it can be chosen. Those contracts are effectively decided; a large percentage gap there is mostly noise, and the payoff is asymmetric.
  4. Keep only the single largest divergence. Among the surviving rungs the one with the biggest |diff| wins. Not the sum, not an average across the ladder — one contract per metric.
  5. Require a real edge. If that best rung still has |diff| < MIN_EDGE (0.05, i.e. 5 percentage points) the whole metric is dropped and no position is opened. Agreeing with the market is not a trade.
  6. Choose the side and the entry price. side = YES when our probability is above the mid, otherwise NO. A YES enters at the mid; a NO enters at 1 - mid, because buying NO costs the complement. Position size is contracts = stake / entry_price, so a cheaper contract buys more of them.
  7. Size it from a fixed bankroll. init counts the qualifying positions first and splits BANKROLL = 1000.0 equally across them, storing the result as stake_per_position. The daily run uses add, which only opens positions for metrics not already in the ledger and reuses that stored stake, so a position opened in week six is the same size as one opened on day one.
  8. Mark and settle. mark asks Kalshi for the settled markets in each series the ledger touches. When a held ticker comes back with a result it computes won = (result == "yes") == (side == "YES"), payout = contracts * (1 if won else 0) and realized_pnl = payout - stake, then flips the position to resolved. Positions still open are left alone — the dashboard marks those to the latest price pull when it regenerates this page.

The rules, in numbers

BANKROLL = 1000.0   MIN_EDGE = 0.05   PRICE_BAND = (0.03, 0.97)
pick = argmax |cp_p − yes_mid| over rungs inside PRICE_BAND
side = YES if cp_p > yes_mid else NO
entry_price = yes_mid (YES)  |  1 − yes_mid (NO)
contracts = stake / entry_price
open P&L = contracts × (current_price − entry_price)
realized_pnl = contracts × (1 if won else 0) − stake
A position only ever moves one way down this list. data/portfolio.json is the single record of it; scripts/daily.sh runs add then mark each day.

Why bet a rung and not the number

A tempting alternative would be to trade the metric itself — if ClaudeProphet says $44.2B and the market implies $42.6B, buy “higher”. But Kalshi does not sell the number; it sells a dozen yes/no contracts about it, and they are not equally mispriced. Near the middle of the ladder a 1.5% difference in the forecast can move a contract 20 points; out in the tails the same difference moves it barely at all.

So the strategy converts a view about a number into a bet on the one contract where that view implies the most disagreement in price terms. That rung is where any genuine edge is concentrated, and it is also the cleanest test of the forecast: if the model is right about the distribution, the contract it disagrees with most is the one it should make money on. Concentrating on one rung per metric also keeps the positions independent — two rungs of the same ladder are nearly the same bet twice.

The flip side is honest: one contract per metric means each position is a coarse, roughly binary outcome, and the ladder’s widest gap is also where a badly calibrated p10–p90 does the most damage. The point of the paper book is to find out which.

Where to see the results

  • Paper P&L — unrealized plus realized, the headline number for the whole book.
  • Unrealized — open positions marked to the latest price pull: contracts × (current − entry), summed.
  • Realized — settled positions only, summed realized_pnl. This is the number that cannot move again.
  • Deployed — stake currently tied up in open positions, out of the $1,000 bankroll.
  • Open positions and Record — how many bets are live, and the wins–losses count on the ones that have settled.
  • Cumulative realized P&L — the equity curve, starting at $0 with one step per settled position, in settlement order. Hover a dot for the company and that position’s contribution.
  • The positions table — every bet with its side, entry price, our probability at entry, the current price and the running P&L; settled rows carry a settled yes/no tag.

All of it sits in the Paper portfolio panel on the Dashboard tab. Nothing on this page is a recommendation, and no trade is ever placed — the ledger exists to keep the forecasts honest.
